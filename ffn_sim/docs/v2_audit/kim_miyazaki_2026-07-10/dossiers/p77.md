## Summary
The paper asks whether and how motor-driven **extensile microtubule (MT) bundle elongation** can drive **large-scale directed cytoplasmic flow** (cytoplasmic streaming) and what role a **physical boundary (spatial confinement)** plays. Using **metaphase Xenopus egg extracts** as a cytoplasm model (with actin removed by cytochalasin D and MTs stabilized by fluorescent Taxol), and with **dynein activity suppressed** by the dynactin p150-CC1 fragment, the authors show two regimes. (1) In **bulk** extracts, kinesin-bundled MTs form extensile bundles that connect into a random lattice/network; turbulent-like vortex flows continually emerge and die within ~1 min, with a velocity–velocity correlation length ~50 µm (≈ single-bundle length) and steady flow speed 0.36 µm/min — consistent with purely **hydrodynamic** bacterial-turbulence-like self-organization. (2) When the extract is **encapsulated in cell-sized water-in-oil droplets (100–700 µm diam.)**, the extensile bundles push against the droplet boundary; this pushing force breaks the rotational symmetry of the random bundle network and organizes it into a **rotating vortex (spiral) array**, driving **rotational cytoplasmic flow** that is temporally stable (tens of minutes) and 10–100× larger in length- and timescale than the bulk vortices. Mean angular velocity scales linearly with 1/R (constant boundary flow speed ~3.19 µm/min ≈ the measured bundle elongation rate 3.58 µm/min), and the driving force is shown to come from **kinesin-driven MT–MT sliding** (ATP-dependent; abolished by apyrase/AMP-PNP) rather than polymerization (Taxol-insensitive). The authors argue MTs act as rigid rods cross-linked by MAPs into an elastic network that transmits the boundary pushing force over long range — i.e. cytoskeletal streaming uses **mechanical** interactions with the boundary in addition to hydrodynamic ones. A 5-phase mechanistic cartoon (polymerize→network→boundary pushing→symmetry breaking→vortex+positive feedback) is proposed.

## Model & Methods
- **Model type:** EXPERIMENTAL in-vitro reconstitution. No numerical simulation, no agent-based/Langevin model. Cytoplasm model = metaphase Xenopus egg extract. Two geometries: bulk flow chamber and cell-sized droplets. Quantification by fluorescence/confocal microscopy + Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) + bead/organelle tracking.
- **Extract prep:** Xenopus egg extracts prepared per Desai 1999 (ref 19). Reagents/proteins/PEG-coated beads mixed on ice before droplet formation.
- **Bulk chamber:** 20 × 20 mm², height ~100 µm; extract perfused, sealed to prevent stray flow; warmed from 0 to 20 °C to trigger MT polymerization. 0 min ≡ temperature elevation.
- **Droplets (cell-sized confinement):** lipid–oil mixture (per Miyazaki 2015 refs 34–36); extract added; water-in-oil droplets formed by tapping on ice or at 16 °C; extract-in-oil droplets perfused into chamber of siliconized coverslips spaced by double-faced tape, sealed with Valap. Droplets sediment (high mass density) and flatten in z (height/diameter = 0.465 ± 0.050). Imaged at 20 ± 1 °C.
- **Compressed droplets (2D assay):** droplets squeezed between two coverslips to 5–10 µm height to restrict bundle motion to a 2D plane and image individual-bundle dynamics; observed 2–4 h after droplet formation (steady state). No rotational flow in this compressed geometry — used only to measure bundle elongation/lifetime.
- **Perturbations:** dynein inhibitor p150-CC1 (dynactin CC1 fragment) 800 nM std (also 400/800/2000 nM tested); Taxol (Oregon Green-labeled) 100 nM std (50/200/500 nM variants); cytochalasin D 2 µM (removes actin dynamics, all expts); nocodazole / no-Taxol as MT-depletion negative controls; apyrase (ATP depletion) and AMP-PNP (non-hydrolyzable ATP) to block motor activity.
- **Analysis:** PIV of cytoplasmic granules / PEG-coated 0.25-µm tracer beads / organelles. Equal-time velocity–velocity spatial correlation C(r,t). Angular velocity ⟨ω⟩ of beads. Software: LabVIEW + ImageJ (bead-tracking plug-in; PIVlab-type analysis, ref 38).
- **Algorithm outline (proposed mechanism, Fig. 5A, 5 phases):** Phase 1 MT polymerization + kinesin/MAP bundling (parallel/antiparallel); Phase 2 random bundle network via cross-linkers; Phase 3 kinesin extensile elongation → bundles reach boundary → generate moment of force; Phase 4 spontaneous symmetry breaking (low bundle density makes CW/CCW balance fragile) → whole-network rotation → rotational flow; Phase 5 positive feedback (flow aligns bundles → alignment accelerates flow) → stable unidirectional vortex over tens of minutes.

## Governing Equations
Only one explicit equation appears (this is an experimental paper).

**(1) Equal-time velocity–velocity spatial correlation function:**

C(r, t) = ⟨ [ v(x+r, t) · v(x, t) ] / [ |v(x+r, t)| |v(x, t)| ] ⟩_{x,θ}

where ⟨·⟩_{x,θ} = average over space coordinates x = (x,y) and over all angles θ of the separation vector r.
*Meaning:* normalized orientational correlation of the flow direction between two points separated by distance r at time t. C(r) decays exponentially to 0 at ~50 µm (the "correlation length" of the bulk vortex flow).

**Scaling relation (stated, not a formal equation):** mean angular velocity ⟨ω⟩_{x,t} ∝ R⁻¹ (R = droplet radius) — implies flow speed near the boundary is constant, independent of droplet size.

**Order-of-magnitude estimate (Discussion, not numbered):** required flow-velocity gradient to orient a 21.9-µm bundle from θ=0° to 60° within the bundle lifetime > 0.05 min⁻¹; the max gradient the bulk flow can supply ≈ 0.033 min⁻¹ = (0.36 µm·min⁻¹ × 2 / 21.9 µm), giving ~31.9 min to reorient — too slow, hence hydrodynamics alone insufficient; mechanical (boundary) interaction required.

## Force Laws & Potentials
No explicit force law or interaction potential is written (experimental study). Qualitative force concepts invoked:
- **Extensile force:** kinesin-driven MT–MT sliding elongates bundles (extensile, "pusher"-type active units).
- **Boundary pushing force:** an elongating bundle whose end contacts the droplet boundary exerts a pushing force; the end does **not slip** along the boundary ⇒ implied **friction** between bundle end and boundary. This generates a "moment of force" against the network, rotating the whole bundle network.
- **Bundle mechanics:** bundles **buckle or bend** after reaching the boundary (Euler-buckling-like response to compressive load). MTs treated as ~rigid rods (persistence length 1–2 mm ≫ MT contour length ~14.5 µm), cross-linked by MAPs into an elastic network transmitting force over long range.

## Relevance flags
- **DCM?** Marginal-yes. The cell-sized water-in-oil droplet is a confining boundary (like DCM's membrane shell); the ⟨ω⟩∝1/R scaling and constant boundary flow speed, plus boundary friction / non-slip end and buckling-under-confinement, are relevant reference physics for boundary-force transmission in a confined cell. But there is no surface-mesh/cortex-tension model here, so it is context, not a direct DCM parameter source.
- **FF?** Yes (strong). This is fine-grained active-filament physics directly in FF's domain: extensile microtubule bundles built by kinesin MT–MT sliding, non-motor cross-linkers (MAPs), bundle buckling/bending, rigid-rod filaments (L_p 1–2 mm), quantitative elongation rate (3.58 µm/min) and bundle lifetime (20.9 min). Useful as a microtubule/kinesin analog to FF's actin/myosin machinery and as a self-organization benchmark (extensile vs contractile). Note: FF is currently actin-cortex focused; MT compartment would be a future extension.
